Character dossier specification
Create a canonical character visual dossier strong enough for another image generator to reproduce the character consistently.

DOCUMENT:
1. IDENTITY - name, apparent age, species/type, overall visual impression.
2. BODY - height, build, proportions, posture, anatomy.
3. HEAD / FACE - face shape, eyes, nose or muzzle, mouth, ears, hair or fur, markings.
4. COLOR LOCKS - exact visual color relationships.
5. CLOTHING - every recurring garment and its construction.
6. ACCESSORIES - permanent or required objects.
7. SILHOUETTE - what makes the character recognizable even in shadow.
8. IMMUTABLE TRAITS - things future generations may never change.
9. FLEXIBLE TRAITS - things that may vary safely.
10. FORBIDDEN DRIFT - common incorrect interpretations.
11. EXPRESSION RANGE - characteristic expressions and limits.
12. GENERATION PROMPT - a reusable master prompt.
13. NEGATIVE CONSTRAINTS - incorrect anatomy, markings, clothing, species traits, or style interpretations to avoid.

Creation mode / 05

Infographic

Information first. Decoration last. A strong infographic begins with source truth, then compresses toward one dominant takeaway.

Source-groundedOne main message3-5 supportsAccessible charts

The information pipeline

Audit the evidence.Extract only supported facts, dates, numbers, categories, sequences, comparisons, relationships, and definitions.
Define one takeaway.The reader should understand the primary message within seconds.
Choose structure.Timeline, process, comparison, taxonomy, system map, hierarchy, dashboard, hero stat, cause/effect, or before/after.
Compress copy.Short labels, prominent numbers, concise callouts, qualifications intact.
Design + accessibility.Grid, whitespace, reading order, typographic hierarchy, restrained color, readable labels, no color-only meaning.

Pick the visual grammar that matches the relationship in the material. Do not default to a stack of statistic boxes.

Process

Use for ordered steps: Step 1 -> Step 2 -> Step 3.

Timeline

Use when chronological order and change through time are the story.

Comparison

Use for A versus B, tradeoffs, or side-by-side differences.

Taxonomy

Use for categories, families, and relationships among types.

Hierarchy

Use for nested structures, authority, containment, or parent-child relationships.

System map / anatomy

Use when the reader needs to understand parts, connections, and flow inside one system.

Dashboard

Use for several related metrics that need scanning rather than one narrative sequence.

Hero stat

Use when one number dominates the story and everything else explains it.

Cause -> effect

Use for mechanisms, dependencies, consequences, and cascading outcomes.

Before -> after

Use for transformations where the contrast itself is the message.

Infographic or illustration?

Choose infographic

When the main value is understanding information, relationships, evidence, sequence, or comparison.

Choose illustration

When the main value is seeing a scene, character, object, mood, or visual moment.

A dramatic illustrated explainer is still an infographic when information hierarchy is the reason the artifact exists.

Use only information supported by the supplied source or explicitly verified current sources.

Determine the single strongest takeaway.

STRUCTURE:

Inspect factual accuracy, hierarchy, spacing, alignment, typography, chart clarity, accessibility, unsupported claims, and clutter. Revise any major weakness before delivering the final infographic.

1Shot sizeExtreme close-up, close, medium, full, wide, or extreme wide.
2Height + angleEye-level, low, high, overhead, ground-level, tilted only when purposeful.

Mastery / Light

Lighting + Color

Light is not a mood adjective. It has sources, direction, hardness, color, falloff, and consequences for materials.

Source logicContrastPalette rolesAtmosphere

Build light in layers

Primary source.Name what actually emits the dominant light and where it sits.
Contrast policy.Soft wrap, hard noir split, flat overcast, luminous bounce, or deep falloff.
Practical sources.Windows, lamps, signs, screens, fire, reflected sky, or none.
Atmosphere.Haze, dust, rain, smoke, mist, or clean air change how beams and distance read.

Color has jobs

Mastery / Style

Style DNA

A useful style description tells the renderer how the image behaves. “Beautiful anime” is a label. Shape, line, value, surface, depth, and finish are instructions.

Shape languageLine systemValue structureSurfaceFinish

The eight-part extraction

1Shape.

Angular, rounded, elongated, blocky, simplified, ornamental, geometric.

2Line.

Bold contour, thin ink, no outline, broken line, uniform or variable weight.

3Value.

Flat value groups, soft modeling, graphic black shapes, high-key, low-key.

4Color.

Muted, saturated, constrained, complementary, local color, atmospheric color.

5Surface.

Clean digital, dry brush, grain, paper tooth, glossy 3D, cel shading.

6Depth.

Flat poster space, layered parallax, realistic perspective, miniature diorama.

7Light.

Graphic shadow shapes, soft bounce, hard key, luminous ambient field.

8Finish.

Clean, distressed, printed, archival, polished, intentionally imperfect.

Style transfer that preserves identity

Separate what the subject is from how the subject is rendered. The new style may change line, shading, texture, and palette; it does not automatically get permission to change face, anatomy, pose, camera, or object placement.

Poster + Cover

A poster is not “an illustration with words.” It is a hierarchy machine: one idea, one focal structure, deliberate text territory, and ruthless control of competing detail.

Key artHierarchyNegative spaceType-safe zone

The poster stack

One visual thesis.What should someone understand before they can read anything?
One dominant image.Subject, symbol, confrontation, scale relationship, or negative-space shape.

Wonder Lab

Current technique
Silhouette first

If the subject does not read as a flat shape, detail will not rescue the composition.

SILHOUETTE RULE: the primary subject must remain immediately recognizable as a solid black shape at thumbnail size.

Advanced moves

Use one or two, not all

Witness scale

Place a familiar figure or object against something enormous. Scale becomes felt rather than stated.

One forbidden convenience

Ban the obvious solution: no centered hero, no neon, no fog, no symmetrical pose. The composition must find another answer.

Material triad

Limit a scene to three major surface families. Material coherence makes imaginary worlds feel authored.

Environmental evidence

Instead of explaining what happened, show one or two physical consequences that let the viewer infer it.

Controlled imperfection

Define the imperfection: print misregistration, dry brush breakup, uneven cel fill, worn enamel. “Messy” is not a production rule.

Negative-space subject

Let absence form the important shape. This is especially powerful for horror, posters, silhouettes, and impossible phenomena.

Visual comfort

Six standing image rules

1
Independent images remain independent.

No unsolicited collage, grid, montage, contact sheet, split-screen, or tiled canvas.

2
Unified artifacts remain unified.

A turnaround, comic page, expression sheet, storyboard page, or sprite sheet may intentionally contain multiple views or panels.

3
Literal counts matter.

Five images means five outputs. Six comic pages means six page images.

4
Reference roles must be explicit.

Identity, environment, style, materials, mood, and pose can come from different sources without cross-contamination.

5
Current facts require current evidence.

For factual infographics, verify changing information before treating it as source truth.

6
Edit an existing image as an edit.

If one layer is wrong, protect the rest instead of recreating the composition from scratch.
